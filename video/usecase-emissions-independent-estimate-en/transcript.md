# Independent emissions estimates from satellites: Climate TRACE

Full transcript of the video.

Can you check a factory's or a power plant's reported greenhouse gas emissions without taking the company's word for it? To a useful degree, yes. A non-profit coalition called Climate TRACE publishes an independent, satellite-based estimate for about two point seven six million emissions sources around the world, for free. In this video, we look at who uses it, where it fits, what it decides, the limits as of 2026, and how to think about the cost.

## Where, who, and why

Company greenhouse gas emissions are, as a rule, calculated and disclosed by the company itself. When an investor, a bank, or a local government wants to verify those numbers, the inner workings are not visible. For undisclosed sites, or overseas sites that report under different rules, there is not even a common yardstick. So they place an independent, satellite-based estimate next to the reported figure, and look for the sites where the two differ the most.

## What the satellite actually does

It is important to be precise about what the satellite does. It does not measure carbon dioxide directly. Instead, it captures signs of activity, such as the steam plume from a power plant's cooling towers and stacks, or the heat of a steel mill or cement plant. An AI, trained on real measured values, converts how long and how large that activity is into an emissions estimate. So this is an estimate from activity, not a reading of the gas itself.

## The case: Climate TRACE

The case is Climate TRACE, a non-profit coalition launched in July 2020. By 2026 it has gathered over one hundred non-profits, technology companies, universities, and researchers. It analyses data from more than three hundred satellites and over eleven thousand sensors with AI and machine learning, and publishes facility-level estimates for about two point seven six million emissions sources. The gases covered are carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide, and since November 2024 it also reports non-greenhouse-gas air pollutants that harm human health.

## How the data is published

Here is how you get the data. There are facility-level estimates, both yearly and monthly, available through a web map, downloads, and a beta API. Each facility carries a confidence measure by gas and by month. And there are totals by country, by sector, by state and county, and for more than nine thousand cities, available as downloads and through Google BigQuery. The data is published under a Creative Commons license and can be used, including commercially, as long as you credit the source.

## How it fits a dialogue with a company

Here is where it fits, for a bank or an asset manager putting it into climate-risk assessment and engagement. First, build a list of your portfolio's sites and their locations. Second, match them to Climate TRACE facilities by owner name and location. Third, put the company's reported figure and the estimate side by side and take the difference. Fourth, pick the sites where the difference is large and the confidence is high. Fifth, ask the company why they differ, whether it is the operating rate, the fuel, or the scope of disclosure. And sixth, feed the answer into your engagement record. The estimate is the entry point to a question, not a replacement for the company's reported number.

## Limits as of 2026 (part 1)

Now the limits, and they matter. First, as we said, the satellite does not measure carbon dioxide directly; the accuracy depends on how well the training data covers similar facilities. Second, facility-level estimates exist only for the sectors that make up seventy-five percent of global emissions; sectors like rail and fluorinated gases stay at the country level, so check first whether your portfolio's sector is covered. Third, publication runs about sixty days behind, and past estimates are commonly revised in later monthly updates, so record the data version whenever you cite it.

## Limits as of 2026 (part 2)

Fourth, facility and city-level values can differ sharply from other research. A 2026 study from Northern Arizona University, published in Environmental Research Letters, reported that for on-road vehicle carbon dioxide across two hundred sixty U.S. cities, Climate TRACE's values were on average about seventy percent lower than another government-funded estimate called Vulcan, and more than ninety percent lower for two cities. Climate TRACE disputes the conclusion, saying its national road total is close to official figures. We do not judge who is right here; the point is that you cannot treat a single facility or city value as a settled number. Fifth, accuracy falls where little measured data exists, often in the Global South, which is exactly where you most want to check.

## What you can decide

So what does it let you decide? The difference between the estimate and the reported figure tells you which sites to raise with the company. The confidence measure tells you whether to raise a site now or hold it. And the country, sector, and city totals let a local government decide which sector to tackle first in its area. What you decide is who to ask, and what to ask, not a final emissions number to write into a score.

## Cost of this use (estimate)

Here is how to think about the cost. The data itself is free, and you do not need to buy any satellite imagery. If you use Google BigQuery, charges apply only above the free tier of one tebibyte per month. The main cost is people — building the list of your portfolio's sites, matching them to facilities, and comparing them with reported figures. On top of that is keeping up, re-running your comparisons as the monthly updates and revisions come in. No price is published for this kind of use. As an assumption, a financial institution that already has a clean site list might treat the first round of matching and analysis as a few weeks of analyst work, with monthly ingestion after that. That is this video's assumption, not a published figure.

## When it fits, and when it does not

Finally, where it fits. It fits for sectors with facility-level estimates, like power, steel, cement, oil and gas, and shipping; it does not fit for country-level-only sectors like rail. It fits as a figure placed beside the reported number, as the entry to a dialogue; it does not fit written in as the company's emissions. It is a rough guide for undisclosed overseas sites, not a substitute for a measured value at the stack. And it needs a site list with versions recorded; if you hold only the company name, matching is the first wall you hit. Three points when you propose this - ask first whether the client has a site list, never call it a measured emissions figure, and keep the estimate out of the score itself.
