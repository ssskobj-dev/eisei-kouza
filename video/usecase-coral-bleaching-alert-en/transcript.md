# Daily coral bleaching alerts from free satellite data

Full transcript of the video.

Can you know every day where coral reefs are at risk of bleaching, without a research ship? Yes. The answer is to read the heat stress from satellite sea-surface temperature, and NOAA publishes it for free. Its Coral Reef Watch covers about ninety-five percent of the world's coral reefs on a five kilometre grid, updated daily. In this video, we look at who uses it, how the alert is built, where it fits in the workflow, what decisions it supports, the limits as of 2026, and what it costs.

## Where, who, and why

The product is global, run by the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. It is in real operation, not a trial. The users are reef conservation authorities, such as environment departments and marine park managers, and diving and tourism operators. The goal is to see early where heat stress is rising, so that surveys and use limits can be prepared in time. Until now, you only knew the temperature at the points where you had an in-water sensor or a diver. A satellite lets you watch every reef, every day, on the same scale across countries.

## How the alert is built

Here is how the daily alert is built. First, CoralTemp is a sea-surface temperature made only from nighttime satellite observations, calibrated to twenty centimetres depth, to avoid daytime solar warming and glare. Second, the HotSpot is how far today's temperature sits above that site's hottest-month average. One degree above is the bleaching threshold. Third, the Degree Heating Week adds up the HotSpots of one degree or more over the most recent twelve weeks, each divided by seven. Its unit is degree-Celsius-weeks. Finally, the alert level is set from the HotSpot and the Degree Heating Week.

## Heat stress and alert level

The alert levels follow the Degree Heating Week like this. Below four is a warning. Four to under eight is Alert Level One, where reef-wide bleaching is expected. Eight to under twelve is Alert Level Two, where heat-sensitive corals start to die. Twelve to twenty covers Levels Three and Four. Twenty or more is Alert Level Five, the most severe. To smooth out day-to-day swings, the alert map is shown as the highest level reached over the most recent seven days. As a measure of scale, NOAA reported that the fourth global bleaching event, from early 2023 to mid 2025, brought bleaching-level heat stress to eighty-four percent of the world's reef area, with mass bleaching recorded in at least eighty-three countries and territories.

## Where it fits in the workflow

Here is where it fits. It goes in before any survey or use limit is decided. First, pick the target reef, and find which five kilometre grid cell and nearest virtual station it falls in. Second, subscribe to the free bleaching alert email and review the level in a weekly meeting. Third, when the reef reaches Alert Level One, schedule a dive survey with people and dates. Fourth, from the survey results, decide on access or diving limits, moving farmed coral, and guidance for visitors. Fifth, compare the satellite value with your own in-water sensors, and tune next season's thresholds. In short, the satellite is the trigger. People decide the action.

## Satellite versus people

The split of roles looks like this. The satellite estimates the daily sea-surface temperature on a five kilometre grid, and computes the heat stress and the alert level. The people send dive surveys to the reefs where the alert has risen, decide on use limits or moving farmed coral, and fill in the temperature inside the reef with their own in-water sensors. The satellite does not confirm bleaching. It tells you where, and when, to go and look.

## What decisions it supports

Here is the data you receive and what it lets you decide. The Degree Heating Week map and time series tell you which reefs to survey, and in what order. The alert level, shown as a seven-day maximum, tells you when to start considering use limits or changed guidance. The per-area virtual-station gauge gives you a shared indicator to brief partners by sea area. And the four-month outlook tells you whether to secure survey budget and staff before the season begins. The basic pattern is two-stage. Use the alert level as the trigger to survey, and decide the limits and measures from the survey.

## Limits as of 2026

The limits are stated in NOAA's own notes. First, the grid is five kilometres, so temperature differences inside a lagoon or a bay, finer than the grid, do not appear. Second, it measures nighttime temperature near the surface, around twenty centimetres, not the water around corals growing deeper. Third, at high latitudes where clouds persist, the accuracy of the temperature anomaly drops. Fourth, it shows heat stress, which is the condition for bleaching, not whether bleaching actually happened. Confirming that still needs a dive survey. Fifth, causes other than heat, such as disease, storm damage, and runoff, are outside this product.

## What it costs

Now the cost. The data is the striking part. NOAA's products and the alert emails are free, so this use case can run on free data alone. There is nothing to buy. If you only read the alert level, you need no analysis. Overlaying it on your own map, or aggregating by reef, takes a GIS person's time. The real cost sits in the field work, the labour for dive surveys and the boat. And in the measures that follow, the lost revenue from use limits, moving farmed coral, and briefing the community. Those figures are not public, because they depend on your own operation. So free data does not mean zero cost. The cost lives in the surveys and the response.

## When it fits, and when it does not

Finally, where it fits. It fits an open-ocean reef that the five kilometre grid represents well, where what you need is the rising heat stress and its timing, where you can act on an alert with surveys or limits, and where you follow the situation week by week. It does not fit a reef deep inside a lagoon or bay where temperatures vary within the grid, a case where you need to know the actual bleaching, the species affected, or a non-heat cause, a team that wants to decide from the satellite alone, or a need to track changes within a single day. When you propose this, three points matter. Check the reef's position against the grid first. Never say it detects bleaching. And never call free data zero cost.
