# Satellite data for mineral exploration: ASTER alteration mapping

Full transcript of the video.

Can you narrow a vast exploration area before you set foot on the ground? Yes. The answer is to map where rock has been altered by hot fluids, using free satellite data first. Japan's national resource agency, JOGMEC, reported using this to screen porphyry copper targets in Chile. In this video, we look at who uses it, where it fits in the workflow, what decisions it supports, how far free satellites go, and the limits as of 2026.

## Field, who, and why

The setting is metal exploration, in the energy and resources field. The users are resource companies, trading houses, and national resource agencies. The goal is to narrow a wide region down to promising ground before spending money to acquire rights or to send crews into the field. Because you cannot walk a whole region on foot, the first screening is done from space.

## Where it fits in the workflow

Exploration runs in stages. First a wide reconnaissance to pick promising districts, then geological, geochemical and geophysical survey, and finally drilling to confirm the ore directly. Satellite analysis sits at that first screening step. A porphyry copper deposit is ringed by zones where hot fluids altered the rock. Different alteration minerals, such as kaolinite, sericite, alunite, chlorite and epidote, each absorb specific shortwave-infrared wavelengths. Multispectral data captures those differences, so you can map where, and what kind of, alteration appears on exposed rock.

## What ASTER measures

The sensor behind the reported case is ASTER, a Japanese instrument on NASA's Terra satellite, with a sixty kilometre swath. Its visible and near-infrared bands resolve fifteen metres, the shortwave infrared thirty metres, and the thermal infrared ninety metres. A band ratio in the visible and near infrared flags iron oxides, while the shortwave-infrared bands separate the types of alteration minerals. Terrain and lineaments from a digital elevation model are read at the same step.

## Satellite versus the exploration team

The split of roles looks like this. The satellite analysis maps iron oxides and alteration minerals and extracts lineaments across a wide area. The team verifies the map against known deposits, chooses where to survey on the ground, and confirms mineralisation by drilling. In JOGMEC's report, shortwave-infrared band ratios over Chile, including around the Escondida mine, show the alteration zones as colour differences. This was described as exploration in service, not a one-off pilot.

## What decisions it supports

Here is what you receive and what it lets you decide. The iron-oxide map shows the extent of oxidation and the first candidate districts. The alteration-mineral map shows the type and the centre of each alteration zone, and where to put a ground survey. The lineament and terrain map shows structural intersections and the direction of geophysical survey lines. And the verification result at known deposits tells you how far to trust the map in this area. Remember, an alteration map says there is alteration here. It does not say there is ore here.

## Limits as of 2026

The limits are stated plainly in the sources. First, the shortwave-infrared bands that separate alteration minerals became unusable with data from April two thousand eight, after a detector fault that recovery attempts never fixed. So the usable archive is the imagery from two thousand to two thousand eight; recent surface change cannot be followed this way. Second, rare-earth minerals cannot be told apart by ASTER; that needs hyperspectral data. Third, the data is free, so everyone can run the same analysis, and finding genuinely new ground this way is hard. Fourth, it fails where rock is hidden under vegetation, soil or snow. And fifth, Terra is near the end of its planned life, around two thousand twenty-seven, so plan on using the archive, not new scenes.

## How the cost works

The cost picture is unusual, because the data is free. ASTER has been open at no charge since April two thousand sixteen, with more than two point nine five million scenes, so the data cost is near zero. The main cost is the analysis labour, the atmospheric correction, the band ratios, and reading the alteration minerals, which needs geological knowledge. Field verification, the ground survey, sample assays and geophysics, costs far more, and the whole point of the screening is to cut that bill. JOGMEC's own analysis cost is not public, so no figure is quoted here.

## When it fits, and when it does not

Finally, where it fits. It fits hydrothermal deposits such as porphyry copper, where the surface is dry and rock is exposed, where a known deposit nearby lets you check the analysis, where a pre-two-thousand-eight surface is enough, and at the wide screening stage before you acquire rights. It does not fit rare earths or lithium, ground hidden by vegetation, soil or snow, areas with no known deposit to verify against, cases needing recent change, or grade and reserve estimation. When you propose this, ask first about vegetation cover and whether a known deposit exists, and never say satellites will find the ore.
