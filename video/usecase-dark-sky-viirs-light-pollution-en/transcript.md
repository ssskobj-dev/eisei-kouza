## Where, who, and why

This is used both overseas and in Japan, by municipalities, tourism associations, and environment departments that want to protect a dark sky. Dark-sky certification asks a place to set a lighting policy, upgrade to shielded fixtures, and measure the night sky's darkness over time. A ground meter does that well, but only at the spot where it sits. It cannot show how the brightness of the whole surrounding town changed across an area. That is the gap the satellite fills.

## Where it fits in the workflow

Here is where it fits. It goes into the annual reporting and planning, not instead of the ground measurement. First, pull the Earth Observation Group's annual and monthly composites for the area and the towns around it. Use the observation-count file to keep only the months with good data, and line them up in autumn to limit cloud and seasonal effects. Map the year-by-year change for the certified area against the surrounding towns. Overlay the ground-meter readings and the record of lighting upgrades, meaning when, where, and how many fixtures. Then decide which districts to upgrade first, and write it into the annual report.

## Satellite versus people

The split of roles looks like this. The satellite captures upward light across an area on a five hundred metre grid, follows the long-term trend from twenty twelve, and compares the certified area with the towns nearby. The people measure the real night-sky darkness on the ground with a meter, decide which districts to upgrade first, and write the certification annual report. One thing to be clear about. The case we are about to show is published research, not a service already running in towns.

## The study - 98 dark-sky regions, 2012 to 2020

Here is the study. Researchers at the German Research Centre for Geosciences compared ninety-eight areas in or near certified dark-sky places with ninety-eight similar-sized areas one hundred to three hundred kilometres away in the same country. They used the VIIRS day-night-band monthly composites, autumn months only, from twenty twelve to twenty twenty, and corrected for faint atmospheric glow and a twenty seventeen change in the satellite's calibration. The upward light fell by a median of one point six percent a year in the certified areas, while the distant control areas rose by one tenth of a percent. A statistical test put the chance of seeing this by luck at about two and a half percent. The authors call the evidence cautiously optimistic, and say it is still too early to make strong claims. The reserves studied were in Europe, such as the Rhoen, Kerry, Snowdonia, and Westhavelland.

## Limits as of 2026

The limits are stated plainly in the source too. First, at about five hundred metres a single streetlight or lit sign cannot be seen; each grid cell blends all the light within it. Second, the satellite measures the light leaking upward, not the darkness of the sky as you see it from the ground; that still needs a ground meter. Third, switching from high-pressure sodium lamps to white LED, even at the same light output, can make the satellite value appear to drop. Fourth, monthly values drop out under cloud, and in high-latitude summers when nights are short, so you must check the observation count. Fifth, nearby areas share the same atmosphere, so they are not independent, and standard statistical tests are not strictly valid. The result is a signal, not a verdict.

## How far do free satellites go?

So how far can free satellites take you? The VIIRS night-light composites from the Earth Observation Group are free, much of them under a Creative Commons attribution licence, as long as you credit the group. The record runs from twenty twelve, long enough to show a multi-year trend. The data itself costs nothing. On the other hand, at about five hundred metres you see the trend of the town as a whole, not individual lamps, and the ground measurement that certification requires cannot be skipped. What costs money is people, not data.

## When it fits, and when it does not

Finally, where it fits. It fits when you want the year-by-year change in the surrounding town's upward light, across a whole municipality, comparing the same cell before and after in time, and read together with the lighting-upgrade records. It does not fit when you want the sky darkness itself or proof that certification is met, when you care about a single streetlight or one sign, when you try to compare two different areas' brightness against each other, or when you judge a white-LED switch by the satellite value alone. When you propose this, three points matter. Ask first whether the ground measurement and the upgrade records exist. Never say the satellite tells you the sky's darkness. And add a caution on LED upgrades, because the value can fall even when the light does not.
