# Daily satellite ocean data maps for aquaculture feeding decisions

Full transcript of the video.

Can a fish or shellfish farm see what the sea is bringing before it arrives? Yes. The answer is to read a daily map of water temperature and chlorophyll built from satellites and ocean models, and compare it with your own measurements. Farmers use this to decide when to feed, when to harvest, and when to act. In this video we look at who uses it, where it fits, what it decides, its limits, and how the free base data and the paid service split.

## Where, who, and why

The use is worldwide, with example users in Japan and in South Australia. The buyer is the farm itself - fish, shellfish and seaweed operators. The reason is simple - the sea decides production. When high water temperature lasts, fish weaken; when a red tide approaches, you must decide whether to harvest early or stop feeding. On-site gear lowered into the water measures only around your own cages. It cannot tell you what is approaching from outside the bay. A daily satellite and ocean-model map fills that gap.

## Where it fits in the daily workflow

Here is where it fits - inside the daily feeding and harvest decision. First, in the morning, read the wide-area map of water temperature and chlorophyll and the forecast up to forty-eight hours ahead. Second, measure your own cages, both at the surface and near the bottom. Third, record the gap between the map value and your own measured value in the daily log. Fourth, if high water temperature or rising chlorophyll is approaching, cut feeding or bring the harvest forward. And over a few months, turn that record of gaps into a correction rule - when the map reads one value, you learn what your cage reads. The map does not replace the sensor. It tells you which days to watch.

## Satellite and model versus the farmer

The split of roles looks like this. The satellite and ocean model map the wide-area sea every day, forecast up to forty-eight hours ahead, and show the direction of change. The farmer measures temperature and oxygen at the cage, decides the feeding amount and the harvest timing, and records the gap between map and reality. The example service, UMITRON PULSE, is not a trial. It launched in July twenty twenty and is used as a commercial product in more than twenty countries. One example user, a sea bream farm in Ehime, Japan, now reads values off the screen into the daily log instead of lowering a meter for each one.

## What decisions it supports

Here is what you receive and what it lets you decide. The daily map of temperature, oxygen, chlorophyll and wave height tells you which days to watch and which way conditions are moving. The forecast up to forty-eight hours ahead lets you set the feeding amount, bring a harvest forward, or pick the day to send a work boat. The past trend, up to two years back, lets you compare with the same season last year and plan seeding and harvest. And your own record of the gap between the map and your field readings builds a correction rule, and tells you whether the service is worth keeping. The map decides the watch days and the direction. Feeding and harvest are still decided by the farmer, looking at the fish.

## Limits as of 2026

The limits are set out plainly in the source. First, the ocean-model grid is one-twelfth and one-quarter of a degree - roughly nine and twenty-eight kilometres - so the inner bay and a single cage are not resolved; several farms in one grid cell share the same value. Second, salinity and dissolved oxygen are model estimates, not direct satellite measurements, so they will not always match your gear. Third, chlorophyll comes from optical ocean-colour observation, which is blocked under cloud. Fourth, the satellite sees the surface; differences by depth need field measurement. And fifth, the case reports no figures on how much feed or loss actually changed - what users describe is less manual measuring and transcribing. Note too that the source does not pin down a single satellite; it draws on many data sources.

## The free base data underneath

It helps to see what sits underneath. The base data is Copernicus Marine, Europe's ocean-monitoring service. It is free to use, for commercial purposes too, as long as you credit the source, and that free provision is planned at least through the thirtieth of June twenty twenty-eight. Because the base layer is open, more than one service can be built on the same ocean data. So when you compare services, the thing to weigh is not the raw data - it is free - but how well a service lets you overlay your own field measurements and how far back and forward the forecast and history reach.

## What it costs (how to read it)

Here is how to read the cost, rather than a quote. The base ocean data is free - it is Copernicus Marine, used under its attribution terms. The service itself was launched with basic use free and the forecast and past-data features as a paid subscription. The subscription price is not published anywhere, so unlike some cases we will not put a number on it; confirm it directly with the provider. On-site sensors do not go away - they are what you calibrate the map against. And the real recurring cost is time - a morning check and logging the gap, folded into the daily report. In one regional programme in Ehime, Japan, the prefecture made the service free to local farms, so who pays can differ by region.

## When it fits, and when it does not

Finally, where it fits. It fits farms on the open sea or near the bay mouth, where the grid value is close to reality. It fits when what you need is the direction and timing of approaching heat or a red tide, rather than the exact value at one cage. It fits when you keep measuring in the field, so the map and your readings can be compared. And it fits feeding and harvest judgments. It does not fit the deep inner bay or a complex coastline, cases where you need one cage's value or the profile by depth, farms that want to drop field measuring and trust the map alone, or any number meant as the basis for insurance or compensation. When you propose it, three points matter - ask about the farm's location and bay shape first, never say field sensors become unnecessary, and separate the free base data from the paid service when you explain who pays for what.
