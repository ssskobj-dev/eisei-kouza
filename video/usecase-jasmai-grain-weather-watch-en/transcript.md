# Watching grain-growing regions with free government satellite maps (JASMAI)

Full transcript of the video.

Can a grain buyer see a drought building in a distant growing region before it moves the price? To a point, yes. Japan's agriculture ministry runs a free public system called JASMAI that turns satellite data into maps of weather and vegetation over the world's main grain regions. In this video, we look at what it is, who uses it, where it fits in procurement, how far it goes, what it does not show, and what it costs.

## What JASMAI is

First, what it is. JASMAI is a free public website run by Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, open to anyone since 2021. It is built from satellite observations by Japan's space agency, JAXA, and the United States agency, NASA. It covers the overseas main growing regions for wheat, maize, soybeans, and rice. For each region it shows precipitation, soil moisture, land surface temperature, and a vegetation index, along with how far each one sits from the normal year. You can view maps and graphs, and download the numbers as CSV.

## Who uses it, and why

Who uses it, and why. The buyers are the ministry itself, grain trading houses, and flour and feed mills. Japan imports most of its food, so a drought or heavy rain in a source region matters before it reaches the price. The traditional sources were overseas agencies, such as the United States Department of Agriculture, and reports from the ground. But those reports can pause for budget reasons, and may not cover the exact region Japan needs in time. The ministry has used JASMAI mainly to double-check such reports. In 2018, when the US department flagged drought in Australia, JASMAI showed the eastern region under strain while the western wheat that Japan imports was growing well.

## Where it fits in procurement

Here is where it fits. It goes in before the buying decision, as a screening step. First, map your sourcing countries and regions onto JASMAI's regions. Every half-month, check the maps of how precipitation and soil moisture differ from normal, and mark the regions that look dry or too wet. For the flagged regions, follow the vegetation index over time and lay the crop calendar over it, to see at which growth stage the anomaly appears. Then confirm against overseas-agency reports, local word, and the price. Finally, revisit your buying timing, how you spread your sources, and your stock. The satellite does not make the call. It narrows down where to look closely.

## The system versus the user

The split of roles looks like this. JASMAI and the satellites observe precipitation, soil moisture, and vegetation worldwide, aggregate them by region every half-month with the difference from normal, and publish maps, graphs, and CSV for free. The user finds the regions that differ from normal, overlays the crop calendar to read the timing, and confirms yield and quality from the agencies and from the ground. It is a screening tool, not a verdict.

## What the data comes from

Here is what the data comes from. Soil moisture comes from a microwave radiometer on JAXA's GCOM-W satellite, on a grid of about eleven kilometres. Precipitation comes from JAXA's global rainfall map, GSMaP, on the same grid. The vegetation index and temperature come from JAXA's GCOM-C satellite, and from NASA's MODIS before August 2022, on a finer grid of about five and a half kilometres. And the yield, area, and production figures are not from satellites at all. They come from United States agriculture statistics, by country and by state. The normal year is the average over 2013 to 2019.

## Limits as of 2026

The limits are stated plainly in the source material. First, it shows only how weather and vegetation differ from normal. It does not give yield or quality, and the yield graph is United States statistics, not a satellite estimate. Second, the grid is five and a half to eleven kilometres, so you read a region's average, not a single farm. Third, updates run about half a month behind, so it is not for day-to-day calls. Fourth, cloud and radio interference leave gaps, and soil moisture tends to read high in deserts and too dry in dense tropical rain. So this is a tool for the trend across a region, confirmed elsewhere.

## What it costs

What does it cost? The data is the easy part. JASMAI is a government site, free to view, and the CSV is free to download, with no price listed. The satellites are flown by JAXA and NASA, and the ministry does the aggregation, so there is no data fee for the user. Assume a flour mill's procurement team starts with a simple half-monthly check of the maps and CSV. Then the cost is not the data, but the internal work. That means building a table that maps your sourcing regions onto JASMAI's, and a person's time every half-month to run the check. Yield forecasting or farm-level monitoring is not included, and would need separate paid data.

## When it fits, and when it does not

Finally, where it fits. It fits when you need the weather-and-vegetation anomaly across a region, when you are working at a national or regional scale, when half-month to month changes are enough, and when your crop is one of the four main grains in a listed region. It does not fit when you need yield, quality, stock, or export rules, when you are watching a single farm, when you need a day-to-day call, or when your crop is not covered. When you propose this, three points matter. Ask first what the client compares against today. Never say the satellite tells you the yield. And set, in advance, which anomaly is large enough to act on.
