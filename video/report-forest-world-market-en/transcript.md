# Forest carbon from space - how big is the market, and who pays?

Full transcript of the video.

How large is the global market for watching forests from space - mapping deforestation and measuring the carbon that forests hold? The honest answer is that there is no single reliable number, because this work is mostly paid for indirectly - through carbon markets, government programmes and new trade rules - rather than sold as one neat product. In this report we look at how to size this market from public figures, who estimates the pieces, who the main buyers are, and what is pushing demand up or holding it back.

## How big is it?

So how big is it? There is no clean, standalone figure for satellite forest monitoring, and you should be wary of anyone who quotes one. Instead, size it from three public numbers. First, the market it sits inside - the European Union space agency, EUSPA, in its EU Space Market Report dated May twenty twenty-six, put the whole Earth observation market at three and a half billion euros in twenty twenty-four, rising to seven point nine billion euros by twenty thirty-four. Forest and carbon is one slice of that. Second, the demand signal - Ecosystem Marketplace reported the voluntary carbon market at about seven hundred twenty-three million dollars in twenty twenty-three. Third, the scale of the problem that drives the spending - Global Forest Watch reported six point seven million hectares of tropical primary forest lost in twenty twenty-four, nearly twice the loss of the year before.

## Why there is no single number

Why is there no single number? Because forest monitoring is rarely bought on its own. It is bundled into carbon projects, agricultural services and government programmes, so the boundary you draw changes the figure completely. Do you count the raw satellite data, the measurement-reporting-and-verification service built on top, or the value of the carbon credits that the monitoring underpins? Each is a different market. And free public platforms, such as Global Forest Watch, sit right next to paid commercial data in the same workflow, which makes the paid part hard to price. The rule for a consultant is the same as always - state what a quoted figure includes, and for what year.

## Who is buying

So who is actually buying? Four groups. First, national governments, which run satellite-based forest monitoring to report deforestation and to support REDD+, the United Nations framework for paying to reduce forest loss. Second, carbon project developers and the registries that certify credits, which need regular measurement and verification. Third, companies that import into the European Union, which - under the EU Deforestation Regulation - must prove that commodities such as soy, beef, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, rubber and wood are not linked to recent deforestation. And fourth, commodity traders, banks and insurers, which use the same data to check the risk in a supply chain or a project. Today, government and compliance demand is the base; companies buying for their own targets are the growth bet.

## What drives it up, and what holds it back

What is pushing demand up, and what is holding it back? On the up side - corporate net-zero targets and Article Six of the Paris Agreement are creating demand for credible, measured carbon. New rules add to it - the EU Deforestation Regulation, whose main obligations for large companies are now set to apply from the thirtieth of December twenty twenty-six, after more than one postponement, will require geolocated, verifiable proof. And public capability is improving - the European Space Agency launched Biomass, the first space radar designed to measure the carbon stored in forests, in April twenty twenty-five, and it entered full operations in January twenty twenty-six. On the down side - concerns about the quality of forest carbon credits cut the voluntary market sharply; Ecosystem Marketplace reported REDD+ credits losing sixty-two percent of their value in twenty twenty-three. And free public data competes with paid data. These are the forces to weigh when a client asks whether this market will keep growing.

## How to read the numbers

Let me put the main numbers in context, so you do not oversell them. The three and a half billion euros is the whole Earth observation market, not forest monitoring on its own. The seven hundred twenty-three million dollar voluntary carbon market is the value of credits traded, not the value of the satellite monitoring that sits behind them. The six point seven million hectares lost in twenty twenty-four is tropical primary forest - it measures the need for monitoring, not the size of the market. And Biomass is a new public data source that has just reached operations; it is not a finished commercial product you can simply buy. Read each number with its boundary, and you will not oversell it.

## What a consultant should take from this

So what should a consultant take from this? First, do not quote a single forest-monitoring market size - there isn't a reliable one. Size the opportunity from the demand drivers instead - carbon markets, government programmes and trade rules. Second, keep three things separate in any proposal - the satellite data, the measurement-and-verification service, and the carbon credit the monitoring supports. They are different markets with different buyers. Third, remember that today government and compliance demand is the base, while companies buying to meet their own targets are the growth bet, not the current floor. And fourth, free public data lowers the cost of the data itself, but turning it into a verified, audit-ready answer still takes real work - free data does not mean a free result.
