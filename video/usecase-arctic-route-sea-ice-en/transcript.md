# Reading the Arctic shipping route with satellite sea ice

Full transcript of the video.

Is there a way, back in spring, to know when the Arctic shipping route will open this year? Yes. The answer is to receive a route-by-route opening forecast built from satellite sea-ice observation. In 2019, the summer forecast said mid-August, and the route actually opened on August 20. In this video, we look at who uses it, where it fits in the workflow, what decisions it supports, how wide the forecast really is, what satellites cannot tell you, and how far free data goes.

## Where, who, and why

The setting is the Northern Sea Route, linking East Asia and Europe across the Arctic. By Japan's transport ministry, it is about 13,000 kilometres, against about 21,000 the southern way through the Malacca Strait and the Suez Canal - roughly 60 percent of the distance. As sea ice has shrunk, the route is navigable in summer. The user is the shipping line's routing planner; the forecast comes from a weather company. The goal is to decide whether to route a voyage through the Arctic this year and when to sail. Because the Arctic has few observation points, confirming ice over a wide area is hard, and that wide, daily view is what the satellite provides.

## Where it fits in the workflow

Here is where it fits. First, in June and July, the planner receives the route-by-route opening forecast. Second, the Arctic voyage candidates are placed tentatively, along with a deadline to switch back to the Suez route if needed. Third, the sailing permit application and the question of icebreaker support are confirmed, by the ship's ice class and the sea area. Fourth, just before the go decision, the latest ice map is checked for the opening. And during the voyage, ice updates are received, and detours or waiting are decided as needed. The satellite enters at the first step and the fourth.

## Satellite versus people

The split of roles looks like this. The satellite observation captures the ice distribution over a wide area, every day, and issues the route-by-route opening forecast. The people decide whether to go via the Arctic or via Suez, decide the timing and which ship, and confirm the sailing permit and icebreaker support. The weather company in this case combines its own ultra-small satellite, launched in 2017, with existing satellite imagery and analysis images from the AMSR2 microwave satellite. This is real operation, in service since 2011, and in 2019 it supported 13 voyages.

## The 2019 forecast and what happened

Here are the 2019 numbers. The summer forecast said the Northeast Passage on the Russian side would open in mid-August, and the Northwest Passage on the Canadian side in early September. Opening here means the satellite judges the whole route passable without entering the ice. In reality, the Northeast Passage opened on August 20 and stayed open to October 21, about two months. The Northwest Passage ran from September 1 to September 29, about one month. The annual minimum sea-ice area was 3.96 million square kilometres against a forecast of about 4.4 million, the second smallest since records began in 1979. So the opening date was close to the forecast, while the area came in smaller than expected.

## What decisions it supports

Here is what you receive and what it lets you decide. The early-summer opening forecast tells you whether to route a voyage via the Arctic this year. The daily ice maps tell you when to sail and where to avoid the ice edge. Past years' open-period records give you the navigable window to assume in next year's plan. And the permit and icebreaker decision, from the route authority's review, tells you whether your own ship may pass that sea area. Recording the gap between the forecast, mid-August, and the actual, August 20, year by year is what sets the margin you build into your own decisions.

## Limits as of 2026

The limits, as of 2026. First, seasonal forecasts have width. In the 2019 example, the mid-August forecast met an actual opening on August 20, and the area forecast of about 4.4 million met an actual 3.96 million. Expect the opening date off by about a week and the area off by around ten percent. Second, the microwave radiometer grid is coarse. The AMSR2 sea-ice concentration standard product is about 15 kilometres with plus or minus ten percent accuracy, and even the research product is 5 kilometres, so fine ice-edge shapes and thin-ice bands are not fully captured. Third, whether you can pass is not decided by ice alone. The Russian-side Northeast Passage is permit-based, applied for between 120 days and 15 business days before entry, and whether icebreaker support is required is set by the sea area, the ship's ice class, and the ice conditions. Ships with no ice strengthening may sail alone only where there is no sea ice. Fourth, ice thickness and drift are hard to read from images alone, and that judgment needs sea-ice knowledge.

## How far do free satellites go?

So how far can free satellites take you? The underlying microwave sea-ice concentration is published by public agencies. In the United States, NOAA posts the AMSR2 sea-ice concentration on a ten-kilometre grid, daily. If you only want to view the distribution yourself, the data cost is zero. On the other hand, the standard product's resolution is about fifteen kilometres, and even the research-grade product is five. And the analysis that judges a route's opening, and the reading of each sea area, is a different job from viewing a map. A forecast and a per-ship decision still need a person with sea-ice knowledge, or a service.

## How to think about cost

How to think about cost. For this case, we cannot give an estimate for paid imagery. The price of the forecast and routing-support service is not public, and the press releases carry no fee. The underlying sea-ice distribution data is published free by public agencies - NOAA posts it on a ten-kilometre grid, daily. As for the voyage itself, icebreaker fees are capped according to the ship's gross tonnage and the number of sea areas supported, and that can be larger than any satellite cost. Because there is no public price, we do not put an assumed estimate in this video. Always confirm the figure with a real quote.

## When it fits, and when it does not

Finally, where it fits. It fits where cargo is carried repeatedly in summer and the timing can shift, where the plan can absorb a shift of about a week, where the ship has an ice class and meets the permit terms, and where the organisation can change its decision on ice updates. It also fits when what you want is the whole route's opening timing. It does not fit where the arrival date is fixed, where you count on the opening day to the day, where the ship has no ice strengthening, where a settled plan is hard to change, or where you need the local ice right in front of the port. When you propose this, three points matter - confirm the ship's ice class and the permit terms first, never say the exact opening day is known, and explain that a forecast cannot be built from free data alone.
