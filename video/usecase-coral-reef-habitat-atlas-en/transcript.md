## Where, who, and why

The setting is tropical shallow water, roughly thirty degrees north to thirty degrees south. The users are national environment agencies, conservation groups, and developers who must assess coastal impact. They need to know where reef habitat lies, and how much of it. The traditional way is diving, boat observation, and reading aerial photos. That is slow and expensive, and in poorly surveyed seas there is simply no map. When each country measures differently, you also cannot compare one sea with the next.

## Where it fits in the workflow

Here is where it fits. It goes in before you draw any boundary. You register for a free login and download two maps for your sea, in a format a geographic information system can open. You tally the area of each seafloor class and each reef zone. You compare the map against any local field data you have, to learn which classes are reliable. Then you pick dive sites by class and zone, and confirm coral cover and species in the water. Finally you draw several boundary options and show how much habitat each one protects. The satellite does not replace the dive. It decides where the dive should go.

## Satellite versus people

The split of roles looks like this. The Allen Coral Atlas classifies the shallow seafloor into five metre pixels. It maps six benthic classes, such as coral and algae, seagrass, sand, and rock, and eleven reef zones, such as reef slope, reef crest, and lagoon. Because every region is mapped the same way, distant seas can be compared. The people verify accuracy with local field data, run the dive surveys, judge coral health and species, and decide the boundary. The maps are a public, satellite-built product, already used in national plans, not a one-off pilot.

## What decisions it supports

Here is the data you receive and what it lets you decide. The benthic map, six classes at five metres, shows how coral and algae and seagrass spread, and sets your survey layers. The geomorphic map, eleven reef zones, shows which zones matter most to protect, like reef slopes and crests. The depth and turbidity estimate, to about ten metres, tells you how far to trust the map and helps plan the dives. And once you tally habitat for each boundary option, you can show stakeholders where to draw the line and what to keep clear of development.

## Limits as of 2026

The limits are stated plainly by the Atlas too. First, the benthic map covers water shallower than ten metres and the geomorphic map shallower than fifteen, so deeper coral is missed and the area may be understated. Second, overall accuracy runs sixty to ninety percent and varies by region. One 2024 study in New Caledonia found benthic accuracy of just twenty to sixty-seven percent locally, mainly from a shortage of local training data. Third, coral and algae sit in a single class, so health, cover, and species are not shown. Fourth, turbid seas and waters beyond thirty degrees latitude are out of scope, which excludes much of mainland Japan. Fifth, the imagery is from 2018 to 2020, so later bleaching, storms, and reclamation do not appear.

## What the free maps are built from

So what are the maps built from? The main imagery is Planet's PlanetScope, while depth and turbidity come from Europe's free Sentinel-2, with the United States' Landsat-8 filling gaps. The global shallow-reef map was completed in September 2021 and updated to a more accurate version in late 2022. A 2024 study built on it estimated the world's shallow reefs at three hundred forty-eight thousand square kilometres, with coral habitat at about eighty thousand. The key point for a buyer is simple. The finished maps are free, so you do not purchase satellite imagery to use them.

## Where the cost actually falls

Here is where the cost actually falls. The maps themselves are free. You register for a login and download any area you like, and downloads stay available for thirty days. The real spend is field verification and dive surveys, plus the staff time to tally habitat and draw boundary options. Those field costs depend on the sea and the scale, and are not published, so we do not estimate a figure here. One more thing to watch is licensing. The maps and statistics are released under Creative Commons BY four point zero, but the Planet image mosaic is non-commercial, and reproducing the entire global dataset needs prior written consent. For client deliverables, confirm which terms apply with the operator.

## When it fits, and when it does not

Finally, where it fits. It fits clear, shallow, tropical water, when your goal is an overview, comparing boundary options, or choosing survey sites, when the 2018 to 2020 state is good enough, and when you can check the map against local field data. It does not fit turbid coasts, reefs deeper than fifteen metres, or waters beyond thirty degrees latitude. It does not fit when you need live coral cover, health, or species, when you need recent damage, or when you would treat the map as final truth. When you propose this, three points matter. First, check whether the target sea is even in range. Second, never say satellites reveal coral health; they show the spread of a coral-algae class. Third, give accuracy as a range, and write into the proposal how local verification will be done.
