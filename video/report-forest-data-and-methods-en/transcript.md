## The sensors that do the work

Start with the sensors. Optical sensors read colour and reflectance, like a camera, so they show where green forest has turned to bare ground. The workhorses here are mid-resolution and free - Europe's Sentinel-2 at ten metres, and the United States Landsat at about thirty. But optical cannot see through cloud, and the tropics are cloudy for months. That is where radar comes in. Longer L-band radar sees straight through cloud and rain - Japan's ALOS-2 is the example, and we will come back to it. Optical and radar tell you where forest was lost. They do not tell you how much carbon that forest held. For that, two newer methods measure the structure of the forest itself - NASA's GEDI, a laser, or lidar, instrument on the International Space Station that measures canopy height and above-ground biomass, and the European Space Agency's Biomass mission, launched in April twenty twenty-five, the first satellite to carry a P-band radar whose long wavelength reaches through the canopy to the woody mass below. And where you need to resolve a single small plot, there is commercial high-resolution optical, finer than one metre.

## Free data, and where paid data begins

Now the data. A surprising share of it is free and open. The European Copernicus programme gives away Sentinel-2 optical imagery, and the United States has offered Landsat free since two thousand eight. The World Resources Institute runs Global Forest Watch, a public map of forest loss built on University of Maryland analysis. And Japan's space agency and its aid agency run J-J-FAST, a free alert service built on ALOS-2 radar, which we will see next. This free data is excellent for watching wide areas and long-term trends. You start paying when you need something free data cannot give - high-resolution optical finer than one metre to resolve a single farm plot, tasking, which means ordering a fresh image of a specific place at a specific time, or a commercial service that packages the analysis for you. The rule of thumb is simple - free data for screening, paid data for proof.

## How the analysis runs

So how does the analysis actually run? Think of it in steps. First, set a baseline - a reference date. For Europe's deforestation rule that date is fixed in law - the end of twenty twenty. Second, detect change - compare optical images over time to find where forest became bare ground, and bring in L-band radar wherever cloud blocks the optical view. Japan's J-J-FAST does exactly this - ALOS-2 radar over seventy-eight tropical countries, refreshed about every one and a half months, flagging suspected clearing even through the rainy season. That is step three, the alert and the screening map. For carbon you need a fourth step - weight. Area alone does not tell you the carbon, so lidar and radar estimates of canopy height and biomass are combined and calibrated against measured field plots. And finally, confirm on the ground. The satellite ranks where to look and sets the scope; people still verify.

## How to read it, and where the cost sits

Here is how to read these methods without overselling them. Global Forest Watch reported that tropical primary forest loss fell thirty-six percent in twenty twenty-five - but that was one year coming off a record high, not a confirmed turning point, and the loss was still forty-six percent above where it sat a decade earlier. One year is not a trend. Next, the raw Sentinel and Landsat data is free, but a finished, reliable product is not - the cost moves into processing and validation, which is skilled work. A deforestation alert is a candidate to check, not proof that a law was broken. And mapping area is the easy part - estimating the carbon a forest holds is genuinely hard, and those estimates have to be calibrated against measurements on the ground. Free data does not mean a free answer.

## What a consultant should take from this

So what should a consultant take from this? First, start from the rule or the standard the data has to answer - a European deforestation requirement, or a carbon standard's measurement rules - because the regulatory calendar moves the client's budget. Europe's deforestation rule applies to large and medium operators from the end of December twenty twenty-six, and to smaller ones from the middle of twenty twenty-seven, so the demand is dated. Second, match the data to the job - free imagery for wide-area screening, and commercial high-resolution only where you must prove what happened on a single plot. Third, when you build the budget, the money is in the analysis and the field validation, not in the pixels. And fourth, be honest about carbon - present a satellite estimate as evidence to be checked against the ground, not as proof on its own.
